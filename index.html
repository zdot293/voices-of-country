Australia's First Languages · 1788 to today

Voices of Country

Before 1788, this continent spoke more than 250 languages. Today only a handful are still learned by every child. This is the story of what was lost, where languages survive, and how communities are waking them up again.

Chapter 1 · A continent of languages

More than 250 languages, and most of them have gone quiet

Aboriginal Australia was never one culture with one language. It was more like Europe: hundreds of distinct languages, each tied to its own Country.

Each square on the right stands for one of the roughly 250 languages spoken in 1788. The most recent national survey (NILS3, 2018–19) found that about 123 are still used or being revived. Only 14 are strong, meaning children still learn them as a first language.

"About 127" is an estimate: it is the gap between the ~250 languages at colonisation (National Indigenous Languages Report) and the 123 counted by NILS3. The two figures come from different counts, so treat it as approximate.

What happened to the languages of 1788?

One square = one language

Strong language
Spoken by all generations. Children grow up speaking it at home.
Endangered language
Still spoken, but mostly by older people, so it is not being passed on to children.
Sleeping language
No longer spoken every day. Many communities prefer "sleeping" to "extinct", because a language can be woken up from records and memory.

Glottolog uses an international scale that is stricter than the national survey's, so it counts only Kriol and Gurindji Kriol as safe. Four more (Alyawarr, Kukatja, Mawng and Pitjantjatjara) are "threatened": still learned by children, but under pressure. 227 of the 356 (64%) are sleeping.

Many names, many dialects

Counting languages is hard. One language can have several dialects, and different groups may record it under different names. The AIATSIS AustLang database, which communities help maintain, lists 1,183 distinct language varieties.

Queensland has the most recorded varieties (341), followed by the Northern Territory (260) and Western Australia (199). These are also the states where the most languages were still spoken into the 20th century.

Chapter 2 · The geography of loss

Loss followed the frontier, from the south-east to the north

Colonisation spread outward from Sydney, and so did language loss. Where settlers arrived earliest and in the greatest numbers, people were pushed off Country and children were removed or punished for speaking their languages.

Green squares mark areas where languages still have speakers: Arnhem Land, the Kimberley and the Central and Western Deserts. These remote regions were colonised later, and communities kept speaking their languages.

Red squares mark areas where every recorded language is now sleeping. They cover almost all of New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania and south-east Queensland.

Chapter 3 · Who speaks today

More speakers than ever, but a smaller share of First Nations people

The Census asks every household which language it uses at home. Two trends move in opposite directions.

The number of speakers rose by more than half between 2001 and 2021. The number of people identifying as Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander grew even faster, so the share who speak an Indigenous language at home fell, from 16.4% in 1991 to 9.5% in 2021.

The Northern Territory is the heartland of spoken languages Hover a state to compare years

% of each state's Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people who speak an Indigenous language at home

In the NT, nearly 6 in 10 First Nations people (58.5%) speak an Indigenous language at home. In New South Wales it is under 2 in 100. Between 2016 and 2021 the share more than doubled in NSW (0.8% → 1.9%) and Tasmania (0.3% → 0.6%), a sign of revival in the south-east.

Strongholds in remote communities

The ten communities with the most speakers are all in the north. Seven are in the NT. Maningrida in Arnhem Land leads, with 2,198 people speaking an Indigenous language at home. Maningrida is also one of the most multilingual places in the world: 15 languages are spoken or signed there every day.

In these places, an Indigenous language is the everyday language of shops, sport and family, and English is learned second.

What do the 76,978 speakers speak?

Rectangle area = number of speakers (Census 2021). Hover for exact numbers.

The two most-spoken single languages are new contact languages. "Cape York Peninsula languages (grouped)" is a Census category that combines several smaller languages, and "Other contact languages" (27 speakers) is what is left of the 15,026 contact-language speakers after Yumplatok and Kriol, mainly Gurindji Kriol and Light Warlpiri. Yumplatok (Torres Strait Creole, 7,596 speakers) and Kriol (7,403) are full languages that First Nations people created after colonisation by mixing English with traditional languages. They are now the most widely spoken Indigenous languages in Australia. Traditional languages still have more than 52,000 speakers combined, spread across about 150 languages.

Chapter 4 · Will the next generation speak?

A language survives only if children learn it

Most Indigenous languages have very few speakers. In 2021, 78 languages counted in the Census had no speakers aged under 15.

Read the grid from top to bottom: bigger languages are at the top. The darkest cells are near the bottom, where most languages sit, with 50 or fewer speakers each.

There is some good news. The number of languages with 1,001 to 5,000 speakers almost doubled between 2016 and 2021 (9 → 17), and the number of languages with no speakers at all fell from 60 in 2011 to 50 in 2021.

Speakers are young

Age of people who speak an Indigenous language at home

Where languages are still spoken, they are spoken by the young. In 2021 one in three speakers (32.7%) was a child aged 0–14, and the median speaker was just 27 years old.

This is the clearest sign of hope in the data: in strong communities, languages are still being passed from parents to children.

The national surveys: fewer languages, but the decline has paused

Results of the three National Indigenous Languages Surveys (NILS)

Between the first and second surveys, 25 fewer languages were reported as spoken and five strong languages weakened. The third survey found slightly more of both, partly because more communities are now reviving their languages.

Chapter 5 · Waking languages up

From loss to recognition

For almost two centuries after 1788, governments made no effort to protect Indigenous languages. Support began in the 1970s, but it has not been steady.

Milestones in language loss and revival Hover for detail

Not drawn to scale: there is a gap of 185 years between the first two events. Hover a milestone to see its source.

Revival shows up in the south-east

Between the 2016 and 2021 Censuses, the share of First Nations people speaking an Indigenous language at home rose in six of the eight states and territories. The biggest rises were in New South Wales and the ACT, in the south-east, where Chapter 2 showed that almost every traditional language had gone to sleep.

It fell slightly in the two heartlands, the Northern Territory and Western Australia, even though their number of speakers is still by far the largest.

Each square is one state or territory, all the same size and placed roughly where it sits on the map. "Pts" means percentage points: NSW going from 0.8% to 1.9% is a rise of 1.1 points. Some of the rise may also come from changes in how people identify and how the Census records languages.

Change in the share speaking an Indigenous language, 2016 → 2021

Green = rising, red = falling. Hover a square for exact values.

Today, revival is led by communities. Language centres record Elders, write dictionaries and teach languages in schools. NILS3 found at least 31 languages being reawakened. Kaurna, the language of the Adelaide Plains, has been taught in schools since 1992 and at the University of Adelaide since 1997. Wiradjuri, from central New South Wales, is now taught at Charles Sturt University.

Closing the Gap Target 16 now commits Australian governments to a sustained increase in the number and strength of Indigenous languages by 2031. The 2026 Census and the fourth national languages survey (NILS4, 2025–26) will show whether that is happening.

Every language is a way of knowing Country

Most of the languages of 1788 have gone quiet, and loss followed the path of colonisation. But the number of speakers is growing, children in the north are still learning their languages, and in the south-east sleeping languages are being woken up. A language is never truly gone while its community wants it back.
